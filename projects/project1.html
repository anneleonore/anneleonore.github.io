Feedback to men: more positive on getting ahead, more negative on getting along

Step 2 · Create reference points for the traits

Computers can’t understand words directly, so a language model (BERT) reads each trait in the same sentence and turns every word into a long list of numbers, an embedding.

In the review, the manager described this person as ambitious → [0.71, −0.36, 0.15, …]

We then average the trait embeddings within each group. This gives four reference points:

  1. Positive agency
  2. Negative agency
  3. Positive communion
  4. Negative communion

Step 3 · Create embeddings for employee mentions

Next, we turn to the feedback itself and do the same. BERT reads each sentence, and we keep the embedding of every word that refers to the employee, such as "she", "he", "you" or "your". These embeddings capture the context in which the employee is mentioned.

"She should be more confident when presenting to clients." → [0.18, 0.42, −0.09, …]
"He could involve his team more in decisions." → [0.64, −0.21, 0.33, …]

Example sentences, not from the data.

Step 4 · Compare with the reference points

We measure how close each employee mention is in meaning to the four reference points, using cosine similarity. For each mention, this gives four scores, which we combine into two net scores:

Net agency = positive agency − negative agency
Net communion = positive communion − negative communion

A higher score means the mention is closer in meaning to positive than to negative traits.

Step 5 · Test for gender differences

We average the net scores of all employee mentions within each feedback entry, then run two tests:

Main test Compare female and male employees, using multilevel models that also account for the manager’s gender.
Robustness check Repeat the test using only mentions that are the same for women and men, such as “you” and “your”. This rules out that “he” and “she” simply differ as words.

Step 6 · Result

Compared to feedback to women, feedback to men is more positive on getting ahead (agency) and less positive on getting along (communion).

Net agency: higher for men (β = 0.13, 95% CI [0.11, 0.16])
Net communion: lower for men (β = −0.19, 95% CI [−0.21, −0.16])

The differences are small, hold for gender-neutral mentions, and do not depend on the manager's gender.

"Positive" and "negative" refer to closeness to positive or negative traits, not to praise or criticism.

Women are told to be more confident, men what to improve

Step 1 · Find the employee

In every sentence, the words referring to the employee are located: "she", "he", "you", "your" or the employee's (anonymised) name.

"She should feel more confident when presenting."
"He can improve his planning."

Example sentences, not from the data.

Step 2 · Read the grammar

A transformer-based grammar parser (spaCy) finds the words that describe the employee: adjectives about them and verbs they are the subject or object of.

"She should feel more confident when presenting." → verb: feel · adjective: confident
"He can improve his planning." → verb: improve

Step 3 · Standardise the words

Each word is reduced to its base form, so variants count as one word.

feels, feeling, felt → feel
improves, improving, improved → improve
leads, leading, led → lead

Spelling errors are corrected, and words used fewer than 10 times are left out.

comunicate → communicate

Step 4 · Find the distinctive words

For each word, a statistical method (weighted log-odds) compares how often it appears in feedback to women and to men. It corrects for how common each word is, so rare words don't look more distinctive than they are.

Positive score → more typical of feedback to women
Negative score → more typical of feedback to men

Step 5 · Result: feedback to women

Compared to feedback to men, feedback to women more often addresses confidence, feelings and encouragement.

Most distinctive words for women

Ranked from most to least distinctive.

Step 6 · Result: feedback to men

Compared to feedback to women, feedback to men more often addresses what he should improve or needs to change.

Most distinctive words for men

Step 3 · Measure the sentiment

A language model (RoBERTa) reads each sentence and gives three scores: how likely it is to be positive, neutral or negative.

"She has grown a lot as a project lead this year."
positive 0.97neutral 0.03negative 0.00
"Your updates to the team are often late."
positive 0.02neutral 0.27negative 0.71

The model was not trained on workplace feedback, so it was tested first. On 198 test sentences, the model and a researcher gave the same label 90% of the time.

Step 4 · One score per feedback entry

Each sentence gets one sentiment score: the positive score minus the negative score. Scores range from −1 (very negative) to +1 (very positive).

Positive example 0.97 − 0.00 → +0.97
Negative example 0.02 − 0.71 → −0.69

The scores of all sentences in an entry are then averaged, giving one score per entry.

Step 5 · Add the performance ratings

Besides the written feedback, managers can give a performance rating from 1 to 4. About 19,000 entries have both.

1 · lowest234 · highest

This shows whether more positive feedback goes together with a higher rating.

Step 6 · Result

1 · Sentiment Feedback to women is slightly more positive than feedback to men.
Sentiment score: lower for men (β = −0.10, 95% CI [−0.12, −0.07])
2 · Sentiment and rating For both women and men, more positive feedback goes with a higher rating, but the link is weaker for women.
Link with rating: stronger for men (β = 0.03, 95% CI [0.01, 0.06])

Both differences are small.

Female managers write longer and warmer feedback, especially at first

Step 1 · Compare the writers

Here the focus is on who writes the feedback: female or male managers. The analysis takes the employee's gender into account, so the two effects are not mixed up.

Step 2 · Measure the length

A language-processing tool (spaCy) counts the words in each piece of feedback, and the average length is compared between female and male managers.

Step 3 · Measure the warmth

Two language models read every sentence:

  • Sentiment (RoBERTa): how positive or negative the feedback is
  • Emotions (GoEmotions): which of 28 emotions it expresses, such as caring or admiration
"I want to support you as you grow into this role." → positive · caring

Example sentence, not from the data.

Step 4 · Follow the same pairs over time

For about 2,200 manager–employee pairs, feedback was available for three review cycles in a row. Following these pairs shows whether the differences between female and male managers stay the same, grow or fade over time.

About this study

Do managers describe women and men differently? I used natural language processing to analyse their feedback on employee development. I did this in a research partnership with Culture Amp, a company that provides employee feedback and engagement software.

Methods: natural language processing (NLP), large language models (LLMs), transformer models, embeddings, sentiment analysis, text classification, text clustering, text analytics, model evaluation, data cleaning, statistical modelling (mixed-effects and longitudinal models)

Tools: Python, Hugging Face Transformers (BERT, RoBERTa, GoEmotions), Sentence Transformers, spaCy, NLTK, Gemini, Jupyter, AWS, Saturn Cloud

Credits: PhD research at the University of Melbourne, in a research partnership with Culture Amp. Co-supervised by the University of Melbourne (Computing and Information Systems) and UC Berkeley Haas.
